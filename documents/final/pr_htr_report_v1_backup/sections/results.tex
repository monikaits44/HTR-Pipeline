\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Reproducing the Per-Character Attention Visualization}
\label{sec:res_bm}

Before presenting the attention comparisons across register counts, the vocabulary needs setting. The \emph{CTC peak column} $t_c$ for a recognized character $c$ is the timestep at which the greedy decoder emits that character; typically only about six of the $128$ timesteps in an IAM line carry non-blank probability mass, with the rest dominated by the CTC blank symbol. All per-character attention overlays in this section are queried at these $t_c$ columns.

Following the visualization of Nikolaidou~\etal~\cite{nikolaidou2024beyondmem} Fig.~5, for each character $c$ the attention row from the corresponding patch-token query at the final Transformer layer is upsampled to the input resolution and overlaid on the input word crop with the \emph{inferno} colormap. Figure~\ref{fig:bm_booklet} shows the reproduction on the word ``booklet''. The top panel is the input; the six lower panels are the per-character attention overlays for the recognized letters \texttt{b}, \texttt{o}, \texttt{k}, \texttt{l}, \texttt{e}, \texttt{t} at their CTC peak columns. The peak shifts left-to-right along the word as the character index advances, and each map places substantial mass on the ink strokes of the character being decoded. The qualitative behaviour Nikolaidou~\etal\ report in their Fig.~5 is reproduced end-to-end on the trained model.

Two caveats already flagged in Section~\ref{sec:rw_interp} are visible in the figure. First, the peak is centered near --- but not exactly on --- the character stroke, and each overlay retains a spatially arbitrary secondary lobe. Second, the reference visualization comes from a cross-attention U-Net with dedicated text-token queries; ours comes from CTC self-attention with patch-token queries only. Section~\ref{sec:res_claim} quantifies how large these effects are and how much of the gap the register count closes.

\subsection{Does the Register-Token Claim Hold in the CTC HTR Setting?}
\label{sec:res_claim}

Darcet~\etal~\cite{darcet2024registers} make a two-part claim for classification and self-supervised ViTs: (i)~register tokens leave downstream accuracy unchanged, and (ii)~register tokens improve attention interpretability by absorbing the sink pattern that would otherwise contaminate patch attention. This subsection asks whether both halves transfer when the loss changes from a per-image classification objective to a per-frame CTC objective.

\subsubsection{Recognition half of the claim: transfers cleanly}

The recognition half transfers without qualification. On IAM the CER range across $R \in \{0, \ldots, 16\}$ is $0.24\%$ without SWA and $0.10\%$ with the SWA-averaged model (Table~\ref{tab:register_sweep}). The seed-noise floor of $\pm 0.08\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:res_seeds}) means the observed range is at most three times the noise floor and cannot be resolved as a monotonic register effect. On READ2016 (Section~\ref{sec:res_read2016}) the range tightens further to $0.09\%$, corroborating the IAM finding on a different script and vocabulary. This is exactly the behaviour Darcet~\etal\ report for classification ViTs, and it carries over.

\subsubsection{Attention half of the claim: transfers partially}

Figure~\ref{fig:register_effect} shows the register-count effect on the attention maps produced by the model. Rows correspond to $R = 0, 4, 8, 16$; columns correspond to the six characters of the input word ``talks.''; each panel is the self-attention overlay at that character's CTC peak column, rendered with a shared colour scale across the entire grid.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{fig_register_effect_talks}
\caption{Register-count effect on character-level self-attention (ViT-RGTS on IAM ``talks.''). Rows: $R = 0, 4, 8, 16$. Columns: input crop, then per-character overlay at each CTC peak column, rendered with a shared colour scale across the grid. At $R = 0$ the maps for \texttt{t}, \texttt{a}, \texttt{k} show broad diffuse bands over regions far from the queried character --- the sink pattern of Darcet~\etal, here surfacing on CTC patch tokens. As $R$ grows, the diffusion is progressively suppressed; by $R = 8$ the peaks concentrate onto narrow vertical stripes aligned with the ink columns of the queried character.}
\label{fig:register_effect}
\end{figure}

Read row by row, the figure directly illustrates the mechanism Darcet~\etal\ describe. At $R = 0$ the query for the first character (\texttt{t}) attends broadly across the image; whole vertical bands of unrelated regions light up. At $R = 4$ the broad bands are visibly reduced and the peak concentrates on the queried character's stroke, though a secondary lobe on the same patch column persists across characters. At $R = 8$ the maps become essentially clean narrow peaks that shift with character index; at $R = 16$ the same behaviour continues but with slightly more high-frequency noise. Quantitatively, the character-attention entropy on the fixed evaluation sample decreases from a median of $5.7$~bits at $R = 0$ to $5.0$~bits at $R = 8$; the Spearman rank correlation between character position and attention-peak position improves from $\rho = 0.59$ to $\rho = 0.65$; and the number of heads whose per-head Spearman $\rho > 0.5$ grows monotonically from $7.7$ at $R = 0$ to $9.6$ at $R = 16$.

Where the claim does not fully transfer is the sink-absorption prediction. In a classification ViT, register tokens end up carrying more L2 norm than patches, because they take over the ``global information storage'' role that sinks used to serve. In the CTC ViT here, register tokens carry lower L2 norm than patches at every $R > 0$: patches remain the higher-norm carriers. Fig.~\ref{fig:register_effect} shows the same asymmetry qualitatively --- the residual secondary lobe on the same patch columns across every character is the surviving sink.

\subsubsection{Why only partial transfer}

Two structural differences explain the partial transfer. First, a classification ViT has a dedicated CLS query architecturally distinct from the patch tokens: when registers are added they take over the global-information role and the CLS-side reads clean patches. A CTC ViT has no CLS query --- the queries that read the attention are the patch tokens themselves, so if a patch has developed high norm it is simultaneously a query and a key of the sink pattern, and registers cannot dissolve that self-consistency from one side alone. Second, the CTC loss does not directly penalize an attention map that looks noisy provided the greedy decoding is correct, so there is no gradient signal driving the sinks to fully migrate onto the registers.

A third interpretive note belongs here. Vision Transformers are known to be data-hungry, and both IAM ($\sim\!6.5$K training lines) and READ2016 ($\sim\!8.3$K training lines) are small by modern deep-learning standards. It is entirely possible that at a substantially larger training scale --- of the order used for TrOCR pretraining --- the register mechanism reaches the full sink-absorption regime Darcet~\etal\ report for ImageNet-scale classification ViTs. The current results are consistent with the register mechanism being a real intervention that is under-driven by data at HTR-benchmark scale.

Removing the CNN stem and the legacy row-major layout both collapse the CER by more than an order of magnitude, and they fail through the same mechanism. Without the CNN stem, the encoder falls back to a raw $16 \times 16$ patch grid flattened row-major, which interleaves vertical position into the token sequence. CTC's monotonic-alignment assumption requires strictly left-to-right token order, and this row-major layout violates it: the CTC forward--backward algorithm has no valid alignment between the ground-truth character sequence and the emitted token order. Two independent architectural configurations converging on the same failure isolate this as a genuine causal property of CTC token-formation, not an incidental hyperparameter effect. The remaining rows are quantitative refinements: the dual head is a real contributor ($+1.26\%$ when only the RNN branch is kept, $+3.92\%$ when only the CNN branch is kept), widening the embedding to $512$ hurts because the learning rate was tuned for width $256$, and aggressive augmentation hurts because dataset diversity is already the bottleneck.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning Pretrained Models}
\label{sec:res_finetune}

Table~\ref{tab:finetune} sweeps the fine-tuning strategy for the two pretrained lanes. The naive uniform-LR fine-tune on ViT-B/16 is the cleanest available demonstration of why differential-LR strategies exist for pretrained backbones: the training loss plateaus near $119$ from epoch~$3$ onward, the CER never drops below $75\%$, and the pretrained ImageNet weights are corrupted by the freshly-initialized CTC head's large gradients propagated at $\eta = 10^{-3}$ across the entire backbone. Two-group differential LR cuts CER to $15.09\%$ --- a five-fold error-rate improvement purely from grouping the learning rate. Layer-wise LR decay produces the best TrOCR result at $12.10\%$ CER with SWA, and gradual unfreezing fails for both models.

Gradual unfreezing fails for different reasons on the two models. On ViT-B/16 (large domain gap from ImageNet) it keeps backbone layers frozen while the head trains, and the head overfits to features the backbone will later revise; recovering from this moving-target problem within the epoch budget is difficult. On TrOCR (small domain gap, encoder already pretrained on handwriting) the effect is milder because the frozen features are already close to the target. Both outcomes are consistent with the general principle that gradual unfreezing helps only when the initial frozen features are close to the target domain.

\subsection{Synthetic Pretraining and the Render-to-Real Gap}
\label{sec:res_synth}

Models trained exclusively on the ${\sim}950$K synthetic corpus reach $0.5$--$0.8\%$ CER on synthetic validation but $30$--$46\%$ CER on real IAM test. This is an unmistakable render-to-real domain gap: synthetic renderings have no ink bleed, no writer-specific idiosyncratic letterforms, no paper texture, and no scanning artifacts, so models trained only on them fit rendering-specific regularities that do not transfer. ViT-RGTS transfers slightly better than CNN--RNN from synthetic to real ($30.31\%$ versus $35.45\%$ at $R = 0$), plausibly because self-attention is less able to overfit low-level rendering textures than a fixed CNN filter bank. TrOCR, whose encoder was originally pretrained on real handwriting, fails catastrophically when retrained on synthetic-only data ($75.83\%$ CER), a case of pretrained-representation forgetting driven by out-of-distribution retraining. All synthetic runs were terminated by the cluster walltime before their intended epoch budget, so the numbers are lower bounds on convergence.

\subsection{Reproducibility Across Seeds}
\label{sec:res_seeds}

The seed-noise floor was measured by re-running the baseline ViT-RGTS configuration ($R = 4$, no SWA) at three different seeds. Test CER: $5.96\%$, $5.77\%$, $5.84\%$; mean $\pm$ standard deviation is $5.86 \pm 0.08\%$. Every quantitative claim in this section is interpreted against this floor.
